This section separates what the signals show from what the inferential stage decides. The readings of the first three subsections are exploratory: they describe what can be seen when the models are given controlled and measured signals, and they give the study its direction by pointing to the frequencies the models appear unable to represent. What they suggest is not taken as a result: it is the direction that the Bayesian hypotheses then test, and their verdicts are reported in \autoref{sec:bayesResults} under the rules fixed in \autoref{tab:bayesDecisions}.

\subsection{The frequency sweep}

The sweep is run on every one of the fifteen geometries of \autoref{tab:hfModels}, over the same band and the same one-hertz step, and \autoref{sec:appSweep} draws all of them. What follows is a generalised view across that set, reporting what recurs from one geometry to the next; the figure below shows one of them as an example, not the basis for that view.

Before the Bayesian estimates are reported, the appendix figures provide a descriptive reading of what the models must explain. Across the retrained geometries, dominant-frequency reconstruction appears more reliable over the lower half of the analysed band, while faster inputs often return near $0$\,Hz. Within that lower half, possible holes occur near $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$, and hierarchical band probes sometimes weaken there, although the local $f\pm1$\,Hz task remains stronger. \autoref{fig:rec16-16} shows both readings at the reference geometry, as exploratory observations only and not as confirmation of structural aliasing or of the preregistered claims of \autoref{sec:agent}.

\subsection{Recovering a component from a complex background}

The sweep isolates one tone at a time, whereas this reading asks whether a single component added to a richer background survives into the forecast. Each geometry receives paired trials in which the same background carries an added tone either on one of its candidate sites or at a nearby control, and a tone counts as recovered when the forecast still shows a clear spectral line at its frequency. \autoref{tab:decomposition} summarises the outcome for the fifteen retrained geometries and for the published checkpoint, and \autoref{sec:appDecomp} states the procedure and shows single draws.

\input{tables/decomposition.tex}

The overall picture is one of loss rather than recovery: the retrained models seldom return an added component, and when they do it is mostly on the candidate sites, while the same component placed beside a site is almost always absorbed into a forecast dominated by its slowest content. The smallest patch retains the most and the widest patches the least, and the pattern is the same whichever generator supplied the background, which suggests that it belongs to the geometry rather than to the signal family.

The published checkpoint behaves differently at the same geometry, returning added components more often both on and off the candidate sites. Since the geometry is shared, the difference is more plausibly related to training than to tokenisation, because the retrained models follow the original Chronos T5 training mixture and procedure under a reduced budget (\autoref{sec:appRetraining}) while the published checkpoint was trained with its own data and schedule.

These rates describe what each geometry tends to return rather than what is retained inside the model. A line in the forecast does not exclude energy already carried by the background, and a tone on a candidate site produces a repeating token sequence (\autoref{sec:tokenisation}) that a forecaster can prolong without representing the frequency as distinct from its neighbours. Their value lies in the direction they give, pointing to where the representation of frequency content appears weakest, and whether that weakness concentrates at the candidate sites once background and phase are matched is the question the Bayesian analysis of \autoref{sec:bayesResults} is designed to test.

\subsection{Prediction on the measured series}

On the photovoltaic series of \autoref{sec:signals}, every checkpoint forecasts the same windows spread across the year and is scored on the same minutes. \autoref{tab:solarPrediction} reports the error of the median forecast, overall and by regime of the daily cycle, and \autoref{sec:appSolar} states the procedure.

The error is concentrated in daylight, where the forecast has to follow a changing level, and it grows through the transitions around sunrise and sunset into the day itself, whereas at night almost every model correctly continues a series held at zero. The published checkpoint is the most accurate, and since it shares its geometry with the retrained $P=S=16$, the gap is again more plausibly related to training than to tokenisation. Among the retrained models none improves on that baseline, several cannot be told apart from it and the rest are moderately worse, with no ordering by overlap or by patch size. One geometry, p32-s20, stands apart with a much larger error and non-zero output at night, which points to an issue with that checkpoint that still has to be verified.

The forecast of \autoref{fig:solarForecast19}, issued on a winter afternoon at the reference geometry, illustrates the typical behaviour: both checkpoints prolong the level of the last hour while the measured power begins its afternoon decline, so the error grows along the horizon, and only the wider interval of the retrained model keeps the measurement within its bounds until the end.

The series offers high-level insight into the application domain rather than a test of the design, because the dominant content of every window is the daily cycle, far below the first candidate site at one cycle per patch, and no window therefore carries energy on $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$. For the application the direction is nonetheless clear: content at the candidate sites reaches this plant only through short weather transients lasting a few minutes, and those are the events a forecaster of this series would be least able to represent.

This section separates what the signals show from what the inferential stage decides. The first three readings are exploratory: they point to where frequency content behaves differently, and the Bayesian analysis of \autoref{sec:bayesResults} tests that direction under the rules of \autoref{tab:bayesDecisions}.

\subsection{The frequency sweep}

Across the fifteen geometries, all drawn in \autoref{sec:appSweep}, the forecast rebuilds a pure tone at the right frequency only over the lower part of the band, and above it mostly returns slow content unrelated to the input; only a small minority of the tones is rebuilt within two sweep steps of the input. The exceptions point at the candidate sites: inputs near $f_s/4$, a site of every geometry, are returned at $f_s/4$, inputs on a site often make the output jump, and the reading is least stable across phases on those plateaus. The linear probes behave differently. The local task stays at an accuracy of $0.9$ or above over most of the band in every geometry, including where the forecast fails, while the band tasks weaken beside their own boundaries, which lie next to candidate sites, so a boundary effect cannot be told from a site effect. \autoref{fig:rec16-16} shows both readings at the reference geometry. The common case is thus a frequency that the representation retains and the output misplaces, with the sites acting on the output as attractors rather than holes; this gives the study its direction but confirms none of the claims of \autoref{sec:agent}.

\subsection{Recovering a component from a complex background}

Tones added to a Light TSMixup or KernelSynth background are seldom returned by the retrained models, and the result over a large sample of draws was unexpected. The injected tone is much harder to recover at the control frequencies than at the candidate sites. It reappears in about a quarter of the trials on a site and almost never beside one, and still in a sixth to a fifth once the lines a forecast draws there with no tone are discounted (\autoref{tab:decomposition}; procedure and single draws in \autoref{sec:appDecomp}). The pattern is the same for both generators, with the smallest patch retaining the most and the widest the least, which points to the geometry rather than to the signal family; the published checkpoint returns added components more often on and off the sites. The result runs against the loss that $H1$ predicts and agrees with the attraction seen in the sweep. Two mechanisms may contribute. A tone on a candidate site produces a repeating token sequence (\autoref{sec:tokenisation}) that a forecaster can prolong without representing the frequency; and the forecasts carry lines of their own on the sites, which a count of presence credits as recovered and which account for a quarter to two fifths of the lock recoveries. Whether a loss concentrates at the sites once background and phase are matched is what the Bayesian analysis was designed to decide.

\subsection{Prediction on the measured series}

On the photovoltaic series every checkpoint forecasts the same windows across the year and is scored on the same minutes (\autoref{tab:solarPrediction}; procedure in \autoref{sec:appSolar}). The error concentrates in daylight and in the transitions around sunrise and sunset, while at night almost every model correctly continues a series held at zero; the forecasts examined prolong the level of the last hour when the measured power has already begun to change. The published checkpoint is the most accurate, more plausibly because of its training than of its geometry. Among the retrained models none improves on the $P=S=16$ baseline, several cannot be told apart from it and the rest are worse, with no ordering by overlap or patch size; p32-s20 has the largest mean absolute error.

Content at the sites reaches this plant only through weather transients lasting a few minutes, the events a confirmed loss at the sites would leave the forecaster least able to represent.

All $41$ fits converged, with $\hat R$ at most $1.004$, an effective sample size above $1700$ and no divergence, and no decision class moves over the prior-sensitivity ladder. Before the other gates the posterior medians read as follows (\autoref{tab:bayesEstimates}). At a candidate frequency the forecast recovers $1.35$ times as much of the tone as at its controls, and $1.38$ times once the lines the model draws with no tone are removed, where $H1$ predicts less than $0.8$; the internal state needs $6\%$ more code to read it, inside the $10\%$ equivalence band. The phase scale is $\sigma_\phi=0.006$. The overlap slope is $\delta_O=-0.13$, with $\Pr(\delta_O>0)=0.053$, and the patch-size slope, $\delta_P=-0.05$, is not resolved. The token dispersion dips on the stride grid, $\theta_S=-0.26$ and $-0.38$ on the two generators, but not on the patch grid, $|\theta_P|<0.02$, and the stride sites move as $f_s/S$ predicts, $\kappa_S=1.000$; only three detections support a patch-branch site, so $H3b$ is not identified.

Convergence and prior sensitivity hold for every claim; the predictive check fails for all of them, and recovery for Models~A, B and~C and for the patch branch of D2, which also fails the agreement of its two readings (\autoref{tab:bayesGates}). The models place the stratum means close to the data, typically within $4\%$ of a stratum's standard deviation, but not their dispersion (\autoref{fig:ppcDispersion}), so no claim is reportable. Every posterior is far narrower than its prior and well away from it (\autoref{fig:priorPosterior}), so the estimates are the direction of the evidence, set by the data, and \autoref{sec:discussion} reads them claim by claim.

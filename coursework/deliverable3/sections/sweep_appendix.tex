This appendix holds the descriptive pass that precedes the Bayesian analysis. It covers the fifteen geometries of \autoref{tab:hfModels}, five per page, and each row is one geometry read twice: on the left what the model outputs, on the right what its internal state retains. It is reported here rather than in the body because none of it is a hypothesis test. Both readings average over phase, both use a single signal family, and neither carries a statement of uncertainty; their role is to show that the phenomenon the models of \nameref{sec:four} are built to measure is present in the raw sweep, and on the signals constructed with the generators of signals \nameref{sec:five}. A summary of the observed information is given in \nameref{sec:seven}.

\subsection{How the sweeps are produced}

The measurement is a sweep of pure sinusoids from $2$ to $250$\,Hz in one-hertz steps at $f_s=512$\,Hz. Every geometry is loaded in turn, measured, and released, and the context supplied to each is about $512$ samples snapped down to a whole number of strides, so that no geometry is fed a partial patch. Both readings are computed from the same swept frequencies, which is what allows a row to be read across.

The left column is behavioural. The tone is given as context, the model's median forecast is appended to that context and the model re-invoked, and the loop continues until a window of $512$ samples has been generated; the dominant frequency is then read as the largest magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform of that window once its mean is removed, and averaged over four non-redundant starting phases. Two properties of that procedure matter for reading the panel. The recursion is imposed from outside and is not Chronos-Bolt-Tiny's own generation mode, which emits its whole horizon in one step, so a curve that degrades along the sweep is partly the model reading its own output. And the rollout length fixes the resolution: $512$ samples at $512$\,Hz give one-hertz bins, so a dominant frequency is placed to within a hertz and no finer.

The right column is representational. At each swept frequency the internal state is captured at the output quantile head, and a linear probe is trained on it and scored by cross-validation. Eight tasks are scored per geometry: the seven hierarchical band tasks of \autoref{tab:bandTasks}, after Pagani et al.\autocite{paganiPreliminaryInsightsChronos2026} and unchanged from the probing methodology used throughout, each asking which half of a frequency band a tone belongs to, and one local task asking whether a tone at $f-1$\,Hz can be told from one at $f+1$\,Hz. The band tasks are cross-validated with folds grouped by frequency, so a probe cannot
score by memorising individual frequencies rather than learning the boundary; the local task cannot be grouped that way, since its two classes are two frequencies, and holds out phases instead. Ten
phases per frequency are used here against the four of the rollout, because a probe needs examples
where the forecast needs only an average.

Both quantities are archived as parquet beside the figures, so a page can be redrawn without
re-running the models, and the notebook reuses them when they exist.

The sweep and the inferential stage share the band $2$--$250$\,Hz. That matters for the tasks of \autoref{tab:bandTasks}, which partition it: narrowing the range for either reading would truncate three of the seven and leave the family no longer a partition of what is measured.

\subsection{What is shown for each geometry}

For each geometry shown in Figures~\ref{fig:sweepPage1}, \ref{fig:sweepPage2} and~\ref{fig:sweepPage3}, two figures are drawn, one on the left and one on the right, and each is read independently. The left is a reconstruction of the generated window, the right a sweep of the internal state.

On the left, a point on the dotted diagonal is a tone rebuilt at the frequency it was given at. A
flat segment is a set of distinct inputs collapsing onto one output, which is loss of
distinguishability rather than of amplitude. A jump is a tone the model produces that was never
supplied. The shaded band is one standard deviation over the starting phases, so a wide band at a
frequency means the answer depends on where in its cycle the tone began.

On the right, each cell is one swept frequency and its colour is the probe's accuracy there: green
at or above $0.9$, amber through the middle, red below $0.6$, which is close to chance on a
two-class task. Grey marks a frequency no task covers, which is a statement about the design and not
about the model. The dashed verticals on both sides are the predicted candidates
$\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ of that geometry, drawn from $(f_s,P,S)$ and not fitted to anything.

The two columns answer different questions and are meant to be read together. A frequency off the
diagonal on the left but still green on the right lost the tone somewhere between the representation
and the output, not at the tokenisation. A red cell is the stronger condition: the internal state
itself no longer separates that frequency from its neighbour. Neither reading is evidence of
structural aliasing on its own, since neither is paired against controls and neither carries an
interval; that is what the models of \nameref{sec:four} are for.

\subsection{The decomposition on generated signals}

The pages above sweep pure tones. This second group asks the same question of the two generator
families of \nameref{sec:five}, on the geometry $P=S=16$, so that the reading concerns the
configuration a deployment would meet and the two branches of $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ coincide
in a single comb at multiples of $f_s/16$. The method is stated in \nameref{sec:six}; what follows
is only what is needed to read the panels.

Each run draws one realisation, supplies its first $480$ samples as context, and keeps the
$64$-sample horizon at the median quantile. The real signal is cut to the same $64$ samples that
follow the context, so both sides cover the same instants at the same resolution and a difference
between them is not a difference of window. Two seeds are shown per family, which is not a sample
in any statistical sense: they are there so that a feature of one draw is not read as a property of
the generator.

Two frequency spacings appear in these pages and they are not the same quantity. The transforms are
evaluated on a one-hertz grid, which is the spacing of the plotted curve and is obtained by zero
padding; it lets a peak be located at, say, $6$\,Hz rather than snapped to a multiple of the native
spacing. The native spacing is $f_s/N = 512/64 = 8$\,Hz, fixed by the $125$\,ms the horizon covers,
and no amount of zero padding improves it. Interpolation places a peak finely; it does not separate
two peaks. Two components one hertz apart therefore appear as one line however fine the grid, and
the panel titles that read \emph{resolving power $8.0$\,Hz} refer to this second quantity: read them
as \emph{$64$ samples, native $\Delta f = 8.0$\,Hz, plotted on a $1$\,Hz zero-padded grid}. A
$512$-sample rollout would bring the native spacing to one hertz as well, at the cost of feeding the
model its own output.

Each panel title carries the frequency of the component in hertz, whether that frequency lies on
$\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$, and the fitted amplitude $A$; a component whose fit spans fewer than
one and a half cycles of the window is marked, since below that an amplitude and a phase cannot be
told apart. On the prediction side a line is drawn only if it reaches $45\%$ of the strongest
component of the prediction itself, so the panels show what builds the returned signal rather than
every line a peak search returns.

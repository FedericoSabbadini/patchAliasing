This appendix holds the descriptive pass that precedes the Bayesian analysis. It covers the fifteen
geometries of \autoref{tab:hfModels}, five per page, and each row is one geometry read twice: on the
left what the model outputs, on the right what its internal state retains. It is reported here
rather than in the body because none of it is a hypothesis test. Both readings average over phase,
both use a single signal family, and neither carries a statement of uncertainty; their role is to
show that the phenomenon the models of \nameref{sec:four} are built to measure is present in the raw
sweep, and the summary statistics read off them are given in \nameref{sec:seven}.

\subsection{How the pages are produced}

The measurement is a sweep of pure sinusoids from $2$ to $250$\,Hz in one-hertz steps at
$f_s=512$\,Hz. Every geometry is loaded in turn, measured, and released, and the context supplied to
each is about $512$ samples snapped down to a whole number of strides, so that no geometry is fed a
partial patch. Both readings are computed from the same swept frequencies, which is what allows a
row to be read across.

The left column is behavioural. The tone is given as context, the model's median forecast is
appended to that context and the model re-invoked, and the loop continues until a window of $512$
samples has been generated; the dominant frequency is then read as the largest magnitude of the
discrete Fourier transform of that window once its mean is removed, and averaged over four
non-redundant starting phases. Two properties of that procedure matter for reading the panel. The
recursion is imposed from outside and is not Chronos-Bolt's own generation mode, which emits its
whole horizon in one step, so a curve that degrades along the sweep is partly the model reading its
own output. And the rollout length fixes the resolution: $512$ samples at $512$\,Hz give one-hertz
bins, so a dominant frequency is placed to within a hertz and no finer.

The right column is representational. At each swept frequency the internal state is captured at the
output quantile head, and a linear probe is trained on it and scored by cross-validation. Eight
tasks are scored per geometry: the seven hierarchical band tasks of
\autoref{tab:bandTasks}, after Pagani et al.\autocite{paganiPreliminaryInsightsChronos2026} and
carried unchanged from the earlier deliverables, each asking which half of a frequency band a tone
belongs to, and one local task asking whether a tone at $f-1$\,Hz can be told from one at
$f+1$\,Hz. The band tasks are cross-validated with folds grouped by frequency, so a probe cannot
score by memorising individual frequencies rather than learning the boundary; the local task cannot
be grouped that way, since its two classes are two frequencies, and holds out phases instead. Ten
phases per frequency are used here against the four of the rollout, because a probe needs examples
where the forecast needs only an average.

Both quantities are archived as parquet beside the figures, so a page can be redrawn without
re-running the models, and the notebook reuses them when they exist.

The sweep and the inferential stage share the band $2$--$250$\,Hz. That matters for the tasks of \autoref{tab:bandTasks}, which partition it: narrowing the range for either reading would truncate three of the seven and leave the family no longer a partition of what is measured.

\subsection{What the pages show}

On the left, a point on the dotted diagonal is a tone rebuilt at the frequency it was given at. A
flat segment is a set of distinct inputs collapsing onto one output, which is loss of
distinguishability rather than of amplitude. A jump is a tone the model produces that was never
supplied. The shaded band is one standard deviation over the starting phases, so a wide band at a
frequency means the answer depends on where in its cycle the tone began.

On the right, each cell is one swept frequency and its colour is the probe's accuracy there: green
at or above $0.9$, amber through the middle, red below $0.6$, which is close to chance on a
two-class task. Grey marks a frequency no task covers, which is a statement about the design and not
about the model. The dashed verticals on both sides are the predicted candidates
$\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ of that geometry, drawn from $(f_s,P,S)$ and not fitted to anything.

The two columns answer different questions and are meant to be read together. A frequency off the
diagonal on the left but still green on the right lost the tone somewhere between the representation
and the output, not at the tokenisation. A red cell is the stronger condition: the internal state
itself no longer separates that frequency from its neighbour. Neither reading is evidence of
structural aliasing on its own, since neither is paired against controls and neither carries an
interval; that is what the models of \nameref{sec:four} are for.

\subsection{Planned extension: scope}

A second addition is planned to this appendix: a direct spectral comparison between the signal
supplied as context and the signal the model returns, carried out on the signal families of
\nameref{sec:five}, a Light TSMixup background and a KernelSynth background.

The comparison is run on the geometry $P=16$, $S=16$. That is the geometry of the published
Chronos-Bolt tokeniser, so the reading concerns the configuration a deployment would actually meet,
and since $P=S$ the two branches of $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ coincide in a single comb at
multiples of $f_s/16$, which leaves one predicted set to mark rather than two interlaced ones.

Its purpose is motivation rather than test. Light TSMixup and KernelSynth are the two synthetic
generators of the Chronos data-generation procedure, so a loss observed on them cannot be answered
with the objection that the probe is out of distribution; and the extent of that loss is what makes
the question the models of \nameref{sec:four} are built to decide worth asking in the first place.

\subsection{Planned extension: protocol}

For each signal family one realisation is produced at the probing length, the context of $480$
samples is supplied to the model, and the forecast of $64$ samples is retained at the median
quantile. The real signal is then cut to the same $64$ samples that follow the context, so the two
sides of every figure cover the same instants at the same resolution. Amplitudes are fitted by least
squares at known frequencies rather than read off bins, which keeps them unbiased at the non-integer
frequencies the source pool contains, and the transforms are evaluated on a one-hertz grid by zero
padding, which places a peak finely without adding resolving power. A component may also be planted
on one of the geometry's own lock frequencies before the signal is given to the model, at an
amplitude above the strongest component already present, so that the input certainly carries energy
at a predicted site.

\subsection{Planned extension: what it can and cannot show}

Three limits fix what may be read off the comparison, and each is a property of the measurement
rather than of the model. A $64$-sample window at $f_s=512$\,Hz resolves $8$\,Hz, so the comb of the
reference geometry falls on bins but a candidate and its controls at $f_k\pm\delta$ do not separate:
the picture can show loss, not the paired contrast the models estimate. The forecast retained is a
median quantile, and a conditional median is smoother than a sample path, so part of any
high-frequency attenuation belongs to the point forecast rather than to the tokenisation; the
matched triplet used in the body cancels that term, a raw spectrum does not. And KernelSynth draws
are broadband and non-stationary, so their spectra carry no lines to read against: that family
speaks to loss in general, while any site-level reading rests on Light TSMixup, whose components are
known one by one.

The comparison is therefore descriptive. It is evidence of frequency information loss, not evidence
of structural aliasing, and it is placed here so that the body may point to it as motivation without
any hypothesis resting on it.

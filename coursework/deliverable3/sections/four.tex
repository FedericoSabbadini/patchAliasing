The candidate set $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ of \nameref{sec:two} is arithmetic and holds for any model, trained or not. Whether a candidate is realised as a loss in a trained network is an empirical proposition, and one that can fail in several distinct ways: the effect may be absent, or present but too small to matter, or present in the internal representation and not in the forecast. Bayesian inference is used to test it because it accommodates that structure. Prior assumptions are stated for every estimand before any observation exists, uncertainty is quantified on the natural scale of each effect, and competing formulations, including the mitigation claim, are compared against one another rather than against a null. It also admits a third verdict, that these data do not decide, which for a design of this size is often the honest answer and is not available to a pass/fail test at all.

\subsection{The hypotheses}

Four claims are carried into the analysis. Each is stated so that a particular posterior refutes it.

\textbf{$H1$, frequency-dependent blind spots.} At a candidate frequency $f_k\in\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ the model retains less of the signal than at the neighbouring frequencies $f_k\pm\delta$, which are not candidates. The claim has two readings that need not agree: a behavioural one, in which the forecast rebuilds the tone less faithfully, and a representational one, in which the frequency becomes harder to read out of the internal state. A loss that is absent, or present but not localised on the candidate set, refutes $H1$.

\textbf{$H2$, independence from temporal alignment.} The magnitude of that loss is a property of the alignment between the signal period and the tokenisation grid, not of the point in its cycle at which the signal is observed. A deficit whose size varies systematically with the starting phase, rather than only with frequency and geometry, refutes $H2$.

\textbf{$H3$, sites that follow the parameter generating them.} Because $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{lock}}$ is a union, the claim splits in two. $H3a$: at fixed $P$, the sites at $f=c\,f_s/S$ move proportionally to $1/S$. $H3b$: at fixed $S$, the sites at $f=k\,f_s/P$ move proportionally to $1/P$. A site that stays put while the parameter supposed to generate it is varied refutes its branch.

\textbf{$M1$, the mitigation claim.} Raising the patch overlap $O$ at fixed patch size shortens the stride, lifts the lowest member of the stride branch and thins that family below Nyquist; the claim under test is that this reduces the deficit. A deficit that does not shrink, or that grows, with overlap refutes $M1$.

Each claim receives its own measurement, its own model and its own estimand. All the models are ordinary regressions built the same way: a measurement, a linear predictor $\mu_i$ that adds one offset per group the observation belongs to, and a likelihood. One coefficient is the estimand; the remaining terms exist to keep it unconfounded. \autoref{tab:bayesPriors} gives every prior with the role its parameter plays, while \autoref{tab:bayesModels} in \autoref{sec:appBayes} summarises the models with the value each claim predicts and states their specification in full. The prior scales are varied over $\{0.25,0.5,1.0\}$ as a sensitivity check; all are centred on no effect, so a posterior that has not moved from its prior is itself a report of an absent effect.

\subsection{$H1$}

The behavioural measurement is the forecast amplitude recovery $R=A_{\text{pred}}/A_{\text{true}}$, collected in matched triplets: each candidate $f_k$ with its two controls, all three sharing the same background realisation and the same phase, which is what makes the contrast paired. The contrast is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2contrast}
d = \log(R_k + 0.01) - \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\log(R_- + 0.01) + \log(R_+ + 0.01)\right],
\end{equation}

one number per triplet, negative when the candidate recovers less than its neighbours. It can be formed only because the injected tone has a known frequency. Model~A fits it hierarchically,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2hier}
d_i \sim \text{Student-}t_4(\mu_i,\ \sigma),
\quad
\mu_i = \beta_{c[i]} + u_{k[i]} + u_{b[i]},
\quad
\beta_c \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bar\beta + \delta_O \widetilde O_c + \delta_P \widetilde{\log P}_c,\ \tau\right),
\end{equation}

with one offset per configuration, per lock harmonic and per background draw, so that neither an unusual frequency nor a single corpus can be mistaken for the effect. The estimand is the population effect $\bar\beta$, read as the recovery ratio $e^{\bar\beta}$. To verify: $e^{\bar\beta}<1$ is the attenuation $H1$ predicts, whereas a posterior concentrated at or above $1$ says that candidate frequencies are not attenuated in the forecast and the behavioural half of $H1$ fails. The configuration-level slope $\delta_O$ is the same fit read as a test of $M1$: overlap mitigates only if $\delta_O<0$.

Model~B poses the same question of the representation. At each candidate frequency $f_c$ a probe is asked to separate tones at $f_c\pm1$\,Hz from a captured internal state, and the response is the prequential codelength $L_i$ of that label sequence, in bits. The regression is Gamma with a log link,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2mdl}
L_i \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(k,\ k/\mu_i\right),
\qquad
\log \mu_i = \alpha_0 + \theta_{lock}\,\mathrm{IsLocked}_i + s_{\text{st}[i]} + g_{\text{geo}[i]},
\end{equation}

so that $e^{\theta_{lock}}$ is the factor by which the codelength of a candidate frequency expands, with probe stage and geometry entering as zero-mean offsets because codelengths differ several-fold between stages. To verify: $e^{\theta_{lock}}>1$ is the representational cost $H1$ predicts, while $\theta_{lock}$ near zero says the frequency remains as readable at a candidate as anywhere else and the representation is intact.

\subsection{$H2$}

$H2$ uses the same triplets with one change. \autoref{eq:d2contrast} has already averaged over phase, so the phase index is kept instead and the response becomes the per-phase deficit $y_i = \log(R_{\text{ctrl},i}+0.01)-\log(R_{\text{lock},i}+0.01)$, which is $d$ with its sign reversed and positive when the candidate is worse. Model~C is Model~A on that response, without the configuration-level covariates and with the phase circle cut into eight slices, each given its own offset $u_p$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2phase}
y_i \sim \text{Student-}t_4(\mu_i,\ \sigma),
\qquad
\mu_i = \beta_{c[i]} + u_{k[i]} + u_{b[i]} + u_{p[i]},
\qquad
u_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\phi).
\end{equation}

The estimand $\sigma_\phi$ is the spread of those eight offsets, and it answers the question with one number. Slices are used rather than a fitted curve because they assume nothing about the form a dependence might take. To verify: $\sigma_\phi$ near zero means the offsets are indistinguishable and the deficit is set by the geometry alone, as $H2$ predicts, whereas a large $\sigma_\phi$ means it depends on where in its cycle the signal starts and $H2$ fails. Dropping $u_p$ gives a nested null, and comparing the two by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) separates the size of any phase effect from the evidence that it exists.

\subsection{$H3$}

Both branches are tested on the token collapse $z_g(f)$, the dispersion of the input-patch embeddings across the patches of one context, which is zero when consecutive patches are identical. All geometries share one sweep grid: a uniform $1$\,Hz sweep unioned with the predicted sites of every configuration in the design. No geometry is therefore scored only where its own theory predicts a dip, and non-integer sites such as $42.\overline{6}$\,Hz for $S{=}12$ are covered.

The first question is where the dips are located. Each swept frequency carries two labels, one per branch:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2sites}
\log z_g(f) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
\alpha_g + \theta_S \cdot \text{OnStrideGrid} + \theta_P \cdot \text{OnPatchGrid},\ \sigma
\right),
\end{equation}

with $\alpha_g$ the per-geometry off-grid level. The label coefficients $\theta_S$ and $\theta_P$ are the estimands. To verify: $H3$ predicts both negative, and the two-label fit should win the LOO comparison against each label alone and against neither; coefficients near zero mean the dips bear no relation to either predicted grid. The two labels are not competing explanations of the same dips, since a frequency may carry either or both, but the two coefficients separate only where exactly one label applies, which for $\theta_S$ means the geometries with $S \nmid P$.

The second question is whether the dips move. \autoref{eq:d2sites} remains compatible with dips that merely sit on a fixed grid, so each branch is checked against its own scaling law. Every detected dip is assigned to the branch that predicts it, sites belonging to both are set aside, and the fundamental $\hat f^{\,F}_{1,g}$ of branch $F$ in geometry $g$ is compared with the spacing that branch predicts, $\Delta^S_g=f_s/S_g$ or $\Delta^P_g=f_s/P_g$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2move}
\hat f^{\,F}_{1,g} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
\kappa_F \,\Delta^F_g,\ \sqrt{\sigma_F^2+\Delta f^2}
\right).
\end{equation}

The slope $\kappa_F$ is the estimand, measured spacing per unit of predicted spacing. To verify: $\kappa_F=1$ denotes a branch that tracks its own arithmetic, and any other value refutes that branch, since a site that stayed put as the parameter changed would drive the slope away from one. The scatter $\sigma_F$ is given a floor $\Delta f$, the sweep step, because a spacing read off a discrete grid is no more precise than one step.

Identification is a property of the design rather than of the model. $\kappa_S$ needs a series that holds $P$ fixed and varies $S$, and $\kappa_P$ a series that holds $S$ fixed and varies $P$; \autoref{tab:hfModels} supplies the former at $P{=}16$, $24$ and $32$ and the latter at $S{=}8$, $12$ and $16$. The grid on which these fits are run, the signals they are run on and the order in which the analysis executes are set out in \nameref{sec:five}.

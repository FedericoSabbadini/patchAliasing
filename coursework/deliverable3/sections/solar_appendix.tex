This appendix holds two readings the fitted models of \autoref{sec:four} do not provide. The first is the work on the measured photovoltaic series: what the record contains, how it is treated, and what the models do when handed it. No model is fitted on that series at all, for the reason given in \autoref{sec:five}: it is the application domain of this study and not an experimental corpus. The second is the probe read by depth and by checkpoint, which no fitted model reports because Model~B absorbs the probe stage into a zero-mean offset by design. Nothing here is a hypothesis test and none of the decisions of \autoref{tab:bayesDecisions} depend on it. The synthetic design establishes what a patch grid can represent at frequencies fixed by $(f_s,P,S)$; what follows asks what that arithmetic corresponds to in the domain the report is written for, how the models behave on the quantity the agent of \autoref{sec:nine} would read, and where inside the stack frequency information is discarded.

\subsection{What the record contains}

The source is a full calendar year of measurement at one-minute resolution, $525{,}601$ records timestamped in Central European Time and spanning the whole of 2017. Six channels are recorded: module power at a $30^\circ$ tilt, ambient temperature, global horizontal irradiance, global irradiance in the plane of array, wind speed and wind direction. The five weather channels are present for $99.1\%$ of the minutes and carry a large negative sentinel where an instrument returned nothing, which is mapped to a missing value on reading rather than treated as a measurement. Module power is the channel the study uses and the only one whose absence is structural: it is missing from $62.2\%$ of the records, overwhelmingly because the plant is not producing.

That figure decides more than it appears to. The design of \autoref{sec:six} reads a window of $544$ samples, a context of $480$ followed by a horizon of $64$, and a model cannot be handed a window interrupted by an absent sample. Taken literally, only $64$ of the $365$ days carry $544$ consecutive recorded power samples and $97$ reach $480$. A day is therefore not a unit of data here but a container of fragments. Under the convention of the next subsection the constraint relaxes considerably, because a night stops being a gap, but the distinction between a recorded value and a supplied one is carried through every figure rather than dissolved into it.

\subsection{Missing samples and the zero convention}

Any use of this series has to say what a missing power sample means before it can say anything else. Classifying every missing sample by the solar elevation at its own timestamp separates two populations that are indistinguishable in the file itself. In $79.6\%$ of cases the sun is below the horizon: the plant is producing nothing, and zero is not an imputation but the measured quantity. A further $17.2\%$ fall in daylight gaps longer than fifteen minutes, of which seventy-eight runs exceed four hours and account for $49{,}204$ samples on their own; these are plant or logger outages during which nothing was produced and nothing was recorded, and zero stands for them as well. The elevation test is exact rather than heuristic at this site: on the fifteenth day of the year the sun is up between $8.10$ and $16.97$ hours and recorded samples run from $8.15$ to $17.10$, and the two boundaries agree to within a few minutes throughout the year.

The remaining $3.2\%$ are short dropouts inside a working production curve, $6{,}029$ runs with a median length of two minutes. A third of them are flanked by more than fifty watts and their ninetieth percentile flank is $166$ watts, so writing zero into them would stamp an edge down to zero and back within two minutes that the plant never produced. Two things make that edge worse than the absence it replaces. A rectangular notch is broadband in frequency, and a study of which frequencies a patch grid can represent should not manufacture content that lands on the candidate sites of \autoref{eq:locking-candidate}. More seriously, a zero written while irradiance remains valid and high is exactly the pattern \autoref{eq:inverter-trip} defines as \valueof{InverterTrip}, so the flat convention would lead the controller to isolate a photovoltaic side that never failed. These gaps are therefore filled by linear interpolation between their own recorded endpoints and marked as supplied wherever the series is displayed.

The convention is an input convention for forecasting and nothing more. Supplying a value does not make it valid: $v_P(t)$ is unchanged by it, so a supplied sample still falsifies $V_t$, still asserts \valueof{SensorMalfunction} through \autoref{eq:sensor-malfunction}, and still routes the cycle to safe-mode selection. The forecaster receives a series in which absence reads as no production; the controller continues to receive the observability flag recording that the measurement was never made, and collapsing the two would replace an evidence-quality condition with a physical claim. One further class of value is removed before any of this applies: twenty samples across four days report between $1{,}000$ and $59{,}701$ watts against a $99.9$th percentile of $246$, one of them $43{,}730$ watts against a simultaneously measured irradiance of $32$ watts per square metre. They are recording glitches, and they matter beyond their count because the model standardises each context by its own mean and standard deviation, so one of them anywhere in a window flattens the production curve that window was meant to carry.

\subsection{Reading a minute-resolution record in the units of the design}

The synthetic design is written at $f_s=512$\,Hz and this record is one sample per minute, and the two are reconciled without resampling, because the quantity carried between them is dimensionless. A tokeniser receives a sequence of samples and has no notion of seconds: a component whose period is $T$ samples, rather than $T$ seconds, completes $P/T$ cycles inside one patch and $S/T$ inside one stride, whatever the wall-clock rate those samples were taken at. Cycles per patch and cycles per stride are therefore the same quantities in both settings, and the lock condition, a whole number of cycles per patch or per stride, is the same predicate in both. A measured window is consequently supplied to a model unaltered and its sample index read as the design reads a sample index.

This is what makes the synthetic result transferable to this domain at all, and it also fixes which real periodicities are at risk. At the reference geometry a candidate site is a periodicity of sixteen minutes, of eight, of five minutes and twenty seconds, and so on down the harmonic series, which is the band in which cloud transients live rather than the band in which the daily cycle does. The horizon sets a second scale that matters throughout: sixty-four samples resolve a frequency grid of $f_s/64$, so a component slower than that completes less than one cycle inside the forecast and cannot be expressed there however well it is represented upstream. The daily cycle is far below that grid, which is why none of the readings that follow is about it.

\subsection{One horizon ahead, at three times of day}

The forecast reading fixes a day and a time of day and asks what the next horizon holds. The horizon is the design's own, $64$ samples, which at this cadence is sixty-four minutes rather than an hour; the distinction is kept because the model's horizon is the fixed quantity. The context is the $2048$ samples immediately preceding the chosen time, taken by position and therefore crossing midnight backwards into the preceding days. Two reasons decide that length rather than a single calendar day: it is the context length the architecture accepts without truncation, and it is the window the retraining procedure of \autoref{sec:appRetraining} used, so the model is asked a question of the shape it was trained on. A multi-day context also contains more than one sunrise, which is the only arrangement in which the daily cycle is a component inside the window rather than the whole of it.

Two checkpoints are read on the identical context: the retrained reference geometry of \autoref{tab:hfModels}, trained from scratch under the common budget on the synthetic mixture, and the published checkpoint at the same $(16,16)$ geometry, which is the stock configuration of the architecture and the reason $(16,16)$ is the reference geometry of the sweep. The comparison is qualified here rather than left to be misread: the published checkpoint was pretrained on a large observational corpus that includes energy and weather series, the retrained one has seen only the synthetic mixture, and any difference between them on measured telemetry is in the first place a difference of training corpus. It is not evidence about structural aliasing in either direction.

One day is read at three cuts, chosen so that the horizon covers three qualitatively different regimes of the production curve: the sunrise ramp, the midday plateau and the afternoon decline. A single cut would not distinguish a model that tracks the plant from one that repeats what it was given, because on a plateau the two behave identically.

\subsection{Where the frequency stops being readable}

Model~B of \autoref{sec:appBayes} carries the probe stage as the zero-mean offset $s_{\text{st}}$, which absorbs the several-fold level differences between probe points so that they cannot be attributed to the lock indicator. That is the right treatment for the estimand and it leaves one question unanswered: the fit reports how much the labels cost at a candidate frequency, pooled over the stack, and says nothing about where inside the stack that cost appears. This subsection is the descriptive complement, reporting the same codelengths by probe stage rather than pooled, over the ten states named in \autoref{sec:appBayes}: the encoder $[\mathrm{REG}]$ token after each of the four encoder blocks, the decoder state after each of the four decoder blocks, the pre-projection vector and the output quantile head, in that order.

The profile is read from the archived per-frequency collection rather than from a new measurement, because the cells it needs already exist: one row per probe stage, candidate frequency and geometry, each carrying the codelength charged at that cell and whether the frequency is a candidate or a control. The collection is retrained-weight data built with eighty examples per cell and a context of $480$ samples, and the candidate-frequency construction is identical across the geometries it covers. It is addressed by an immutable revision and its checksum is verified before anything is drawn, so a figure here cannot silently change when the collection does. Codelengths are averaged per stage, separately for candidates and controls, with the spread across cells retained rather than discarded.

\subsection{Band identity by depth and by checkpoint}

The reading above asks a fine question, whether a candidate frequency can be told from its immediate neighbour, on archived sweep data. A coarser one asks whether a tone can be placed in the right half of a frequency band, over the seven hierarchical tasks of \autoref{tab:bandTasks}, on states captured while a model actually forecasts; it is measured for both checkpoints in the same pass on identical inputs, seeds, probe stages and estimator, so the checkpoint is the only difference. What is reported per task and stage is the space saving, one minus the codelength divided by the cost of coding the same labels uniformly, with an independent control in which those labels are permuted over the same features and chunks. Across both checkpoints the controls stay between $-0.03$ and $0$, so no bar reports a probe that has memorised its labels.

Space saving cannot reach one, and the value it can reach is worth deriving rather than asserting, because a bar read against one is read against a number the code cannot produce. Let a task carry $n$ examples coded in $C$ chunks $B_0,\dots,B_{C-1}$, as equal as $n$ allows, so that $|B_0|=\lceil n/C\rceil$. The first chunk is transmitted before any probe exists and is charged at one bit per binary label; each later chunk is charged by a probe trained only on the chunks before it, at a cost $L_k=-\sum_{i\in B_k}\log_2\hat p_k(y_i)\ge 0$. The total and the saving are therefore
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sv-ceiling}
L(D)=|B_0|+\sum_{k=1}^{C-1}L_k,
\qquad
SV=1-\frac{L(D)}{n}\;\le\;1-\frac{\lceil n/C\rceil}{n},
\end{equation}
%
and since $n/C\le\lceil n/C\rceil<n/C+1$ the bound lies in $\left(\tfrac{C-1}{C}-\tfrac{C-1}{Cn},\ \tfrac{C-1}{C}\right]$. With the five chunks used here the ceiling is four fifths, and across the seven tasks it runs from $0.79959$ on the smallest to exactly $0.80000$ on the two whose sizes divide by five.

That the ceiling is not one is the point of the estimator rather than a defect of it. Prequential coding charges the labels together with the model that predicts them, so the receiver begins with nothing and the first chunk cannot be predicted by any probe, however perfectly the representation separates the classes. That cost belongs to the coding scheme and is irreducible; what a probe can do is drive the remaining chunks towards zero, which caps the saving at the fraction of the data that is not the first chunk. The bound is moreover a supremum that is not attained, since reaching it would require probability exactly one on every later label. The practical consequence is that a bar at $0.75$ is not three quarters of a perfect reading but ninety-four per cent of what the code allows, and the figures state the derived ceiling in their caption rather than drawing it, since a rule at that value would fall across the per-bar labels.

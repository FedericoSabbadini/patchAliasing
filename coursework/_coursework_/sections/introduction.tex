Time-series forecasting is a cornerstone of modern predictive analytics, and the instrument through which organisations anticipate the dynamic, state-dependent behaviour of complex systems. Whether the task is predicting fluctuating loads in renewable energy grids, identifying macro-economic trends, or monitoring structural health in industrial machinery, projecting historical sequential data into future horizons shifts operational strategy from reactive mitigation to proactive optimisation\cite{hyndmanForecastingPrinciplesPractice2021}. As systems become more interconnected and instrumented at ever finer temporal granularity, the demand for models able to parse intricate temporal dynamics has driven a rapid evolution in forecasting methodology\cite{benidisDeepLearningTime2022}.

\subsection{Evolution of Forecasting Methodologies}
Historically, time-series analysis relied on classical statistical models such as AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) and Exponential Smoothing (ETS)\cite{boxTimeSeriesAnalysis2015, hyndmanForecastingPrinciplesPractice2021}. These methods are deeply rooted in mathematical theory, offering high interpretability, exact statistical guarantees, and robust performance on stable, low-dimensional datasets. Their reach, however, is bounded by the assumptions they rest upon, principally linearity and stationarity, and by the fact that each series is typically fitted in isolation.

To capture non-linear temporal behaviour without rigid feature engineering, the paradigm shifted towards deep learning. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and their gated variants such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks\cite{hochreiterLongShortTermMemory1997}, map sequences step by step through a recurrent hidden state, while dilated convolutional architectures reach comparable accuracy with a fixed receptive field and full parallelism over the sequence\cite{baiEmpiricalEvaluationGeneric2018}. Trained jointly across many related series, these models established the global forecasting paradigm that underpins most contemporary production systems\cite{salinasDeepARProbabilisticForecasting2020, benidisDeepLearningTime2022}.

More recently, the state of the art has been dominated by transformer-based architectures\cite{vaswaniAttentionAllYou2017}. By replacing the recurrent bottleneck with self-attention, transformers process a sequence in parallel and compute dependencies between distant time steps explicitly, learning localised high-frequency fluctuations and long-range macro-patterns simultaneously\cite{wenTransformersTimeSeries2023}. Two costs follow from a naive application to sampled signals: attention scales quadratically with sequence length, and a single time step is a poor unit of meaning for a real-valued measurement. Both are addressed by \emph{patching}, in which the series is segmented into contiguous sub-sequences of fixed length and each segment is embedded as one token, shortening the sequence by the patch factor while giving every token a locally semantic support\cite{nieTimeSeriesWorth2023}.

The current stage of this evolution is represented by time-series foundation models, exemplified by the \textit{Chronos} family of frameworks\cite{ansariChronosLearningLanguage2024}. Chronos unifies time-series forecasting with the architectures powering modern natural language processing, treating time-series values not as continuous regression targets but as discrete language tokens, obtained by scaling and quantising real values into a fixed vocabulary. Its successor, Chronos-Bolt, replaces value quantisation with the patch-based scheme described above and a direct multi-quantile projection head, reducing inference cost by up to two orders of magnitude at equal or better accuracy\cite{FastAccurateZeroshot2024a, ChronosBoltPipelineAmazonscienceChronosforecastinga}. Patching is therefore not an incidental implementation detail, but the design choice that makes this class of models practical at scale.

\subsection{Signal Representation and Classic Aliasing}
Regardless of the downstream modelling architecture, the predictive fidelity of any forecasting pipeline remains strictly bounded by the digital representation of the underlying signal, and therefore by data acquisition, signal conditioning, and pre-processing.

The fundamental mathematical boundary governing the digitisation of continuous physical processes is the Nyquist--Shannon sampling theorem\cite{nyquistCertainTopicsTelegraph1928, shannonCommunicationPresenceNoise1949}. The theorem states that a band-limited continuous-time signal can be perfectly captured and reconstructed without loss of information if and only if it is sampled uniformly at a sampling frequency $f_s$ strictly greater than twice the highest frequency component $f_{\max}$ present within the signal:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nyquist_criterion}
f_s > 2f_{\max}.
\end{equation}
The threshold $f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}$ is defined as the Nyquist frequency. When a system violates this criterion, either because of an insufficient sampling rate or because of the absence of a proper low-pass anti-aliasing filter, frequency components beyond the Nyquist limit fold back into the lower spectrum and become indistinguishable from genuine low-frequency content\cite{oppenheimDiscreteTimeSignalProcessing2010}. This distortive phenomenon, known as \textit{aliasing}, introduces spurious, artificial low-frequency components that permanently mask and corrupt the true underlying dynamics of the signal. Crucially, no downstream model can undo it: the information is destroyed at acquisition time, not at inference time.

Classic aliasing is consequently treated as a solved engineering problem. Respecting \eqref{eq:nyquist_criterion} and filtering before sampling is customarily taken as sufficient evidence that a discrete sequence faithfully represents the process that generated it, and that any frequency below $f_N$ remains available to whatever model consumes the sequence. This report examines whether that sufficiency argument still holds once the sequence is consumed by a patch-based architecture.

\subsection{Application Domain}
To ground this abstract architectural evaluation, the study utilises a specific physical domain as an empirical reference layer. This reference setting models the solar energy produced by a photovoltaic array attached to an internal load, a local storage battery, and the national electricity grid\cite{levaPhotovoltaicPowerWeather2020}. The explicit operational objective within this system is to evaluate production variations over short-term future horizons, so that the system can dynamically modify the internal load and attach or detach the battery unit, with the aim of maintaining internal grid stability and enabling precise anomaly detection.

Within this infrastructure, a control AI agent is responsible for executing real-time tasks: dynamic load balancing, battery dispatch, state monitoring, active anomaly detection, and internal grid stability maintenance. \autoref{sec:two} formalises the concepts, states, and relations of this setting as an OWL2 ontology, making the link between signal structure, learned representation, and domain meaning explicit.

This analysis is directly intended for researchers, engineers, and practitioners utilising foundation models within the Chronos family for time-series analysis, representation learning, and forecasting, as well as for individuals working in the field of energy management who are interested in characterising the fundamental limitations, structural boundaries, and latent capabilities of these architectures.

\subsection{Purpose and Scope}
This project formalises and empirically validates the phenomenon of \textit{structural aliasing} in patch-based time-series foundation models, using Amazon Chronos-Bolt-Tiny as the target architecture. Chronos-Bolt-Tiny operates with a fixed patch size of $P=16$, a stride of $S=16$, and a context window of $T=2048$ samples as default settings; its architecture comprises four encoder blocks followed by four decoder blocks, terminating in a direct quantile projection head\cite{AmazonChronosbolttinyHugging2025, ChronosBoltPipelineAmazonscienceChronosforecastinga}.

The study aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the relationships between raw signal characteristics and the latent representations learned by the model, systematically identifying the critical links between sampling frequency ($f_s$), patch size ($P$), and stride ($S$), in order to propose actionable solutions to mitigate the phenomenon. The core objective is to challenge the baseline assumption that pre-trained temporal foundation models inherently preserve frequency attributes when processing Nyquist-compliant signals\cite{paganiPreliminaryInsightsChronos2026}.

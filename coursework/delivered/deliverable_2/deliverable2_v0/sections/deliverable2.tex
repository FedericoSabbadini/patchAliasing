This deliverable reports how the plan set out in Deliverable~1 was carried out: how the evidence on structural aliasing was collected from the retrained Chronos-Bolt sweep, how it was turned into a Bayesian analysis, and what the analysis found.

\section{Data Collection and Probing}
\label{sec:collection}

\subsection{Models and signals}
We analyse the five geometries of Deliverable~1: $P{=}S{=}16$, the shorter strides $S{=}12$ and $S{=}8$ at $P{=}16$, and $P{=}S{=}8$ and $P{=}S{=}24$. Four of them were trained with the same $100$k-step budget, so they can be compared directly. The $P{=}S{=}16$ run did not complete correctly and is being repeated. We show it for continuity but leave it out of every comparison between geometries.

Each context is $480$ samples at $f_s{=}512$\,Hz, with a $64$-step horizon, over $[2,250]\,\mathrm{Hz}$. We use $480$ because it is divisible by every stride in the sweep, so the patch grid closes exactly. The $512$ of the probing convention would leave $S{=}12$ and $S{=}24$ with a padded last patch, whose artefact cannot be told apart from the effect we are testing.

As described in Deliverable~1, we use $100$ TSMixup and $100$ KernelSynth signals. Each is normalised to unit variance and carries an injected tone with a known frequency and phase: the corpus gives realism, the tone gives the known quantity. The sine set is kept as the pure-sinusoid mode of the collapse sweep.

\subsection{What enters the inference}
Two filters sit between the collected measurements and the models, and both cost data. The first drops a lock whose control would fall on another member of $\mathcal F_{lock}$, since that would compare a lock against a lock. This happens only at $P{=}16,S{=}12$, where the patch grid at $32$\,Hz and the stride grid at $42.\overline{6}$\,Hz interleave, and it leaves $3$ usable sites out of $11$. The second drops a contrast whose recovery is near zero at the lock \emph{and} at both controls, because that is a ratio of two noise floors and says nothing about $H1$. It removes $57\%$ of the $49{,}000$ collected contrasts, and not evenly: $35\%$ are kept at $P{=}16,S{=}8$ against $59\%$ at $P{=}S{=}8$. Both filters are limits of the present design rather than choices, and they are why some geometries weigh less than others in $\bar\beta$. As a check on the design itself, the collection stops if either level of the lock indicator is empty, because the effect would then not be identified and the posterior would only repeat the prior.

\section{Bayesian Analysis Design}
\label{sec:bayes}

\input{tables/bayesModels.tex}
\input{tables/bayesPriors.tex}

Deliverable~1 applied a two-part framework to $H1$ only. Here each hypothesis gets its own model, and the five models are listed in \autoref{tab:bayesModels}. All five are ordinary regressions. What changes between them is the quantity on the left and the question the coefficient answers. The priors in \autoref{tab:bayesPriors} are unchanged from Deliverable~1, and every effect prior is centred on \emph{no effect}, so a finding cannot come from the prior.

\subsection{Reading the models}
Every model below has the same two parts. A \emph{linear predictor} $\mu_i$ says what value we expect the measurement to take, and it is built by adding one offset for each group the observation belongs to. A \emph{likelihood} says how far a single observation is allowed to fall from that expected value. The quantity that decides a hypothesis is always one coefficient of the predictor. The other terms are there so that this coefficient is not confounded by the design.

\subsection{$H1$: how large is the effect}
The measurement is the forecast amplitude recovery $R=A_{\text{pred}}/A_{\text{true}}$. Both amplitudes are fitted by least squares at the known frequency, over the same $64$-step horizon, from the median forecast. We collect it in matched triplets: each lock $f_k$ with controls at $f_k \pm 0.25 f_s/S$, all three sharing the same background and phase. This is what makes the contrast paired. The contrast is unchanged from Deliverable~1:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2contrast}
d = \log(R_k + 0.01) - \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\log(R_- + 0.01) + \log(R_+ + 0.01)\right],
\end{equation}

one number per measured triplet, negative when the lock recovers \emph{less} than its neighbours. It can be formed only because the injected tone has a known frequency: \autoref{eq:d2contrast} cannot be built from a generated signal on its own. Model~A fits it as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2hier}
d_i \sim \text{Student-}t_4(\mu_i,\ \sigma),
\qquad
\mu_i = \beta_{c[i]} + u_{k[i]} + u_{b[i]},
\qquad
\beta_c \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\bar\beta + \delta_O \widetilde O_c,\ \tau\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\beta_c$: the typical contrast for configuration $c$, one value per geometry.
  \item $u_k$: one offset per lock harmonic, so that an unusual frequency does not distort the geometry it belongs to.
  \item $u_b$: one offset per background draw, one level for each of the $200$ TSMixup and KernelSynth signals. If a result holds for only one of the two corpora, $\sigma_b$ comes out large.
  \item $\bar\beta$: the average contrast across configurations. \textbf{This is the estimand for $H1$}, and $e^{\bar\beta}$ reads as how much recovery changes at a lock compared with its controls.
  \item $\delta_O$: the slope of $\beta_c$ in the patch overlap $\widetilde O_c$, with $O_c=(P_c-S_c)/P_c$. It tests the mitigation proposed in Deliverable~1: if overlapping the patches reduces the effect, it shows up here.
  \item $\tau$: how much the configurations scatter around $\bar\beta$.
  \item $\sigma$ and the Student-$t_4$: the residual scale and the shape of the noise. The heavy tails matter because recovery falls to near zero where the model rebuilds nothing, and the logarithm then gives very large contrasts. Under a Gaussian, a few of those would set $\bar\beta$; under $t_4$ they are tolerated.
\end{itemize}

Writing $\beta_c$ as a draw around $\bar\beta$ makes each geometry count as one unit. The number of observations per geometry varies several times over with how many lock sites fall inside the band, so a plain average over all rows would measure the design instead of the effect.

Model~B asks the same question of the representation rather than of the forecast. Its measurement is a \emph{frequency-local} codelength. At each candidate frequency $f_c$, a probe reads the $256$-d $[\mathrm{REG}]$ vector at ten points, one per encoder and decoder block plus the pre-projection vector and the quantile head, and must separate tones at $f_c \pm 1$\,Hz. The estimator is prequential: each chunk of labels is encoded by a probe trained only on the chunks before it, so a probe that memorises noise pays for itself and gains nothing. The seven band tasks of Deliverable~1 span the whole band, so a single codelength would summarise hundreds of frequencies at once, leaving the lock indicator nothing to attach to. The regression is Gamma with a log link on the codelength $y_i$:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\alpha_0$: the baseline log codelength away from a lock.
  \item $\theta_{lock}$: the log expansion at a locked frequency, so that $e^{\theta_{lock}}$ reads as how many times more bits a locked frequency costs. \textbf{This is the estimand.}
  \item $k$: the Gamma shape, which absorbs the dispersion of a quantity that is positive and right-skewed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{$H2$: does the phase matter}
This is the first of the two extensions, on the same triplets as $H1$. \autoref{eq:d2contrast} has already averaged over phase, so it cannot answer a question about phase. Here we keep the phase of each measurement, cut the phase circle into eight slices, and give each slice its own offset:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2phase}
\mathbb{E}[y_i] = \beta_{c[i]} + u_{k[i]} + u_{p[i]},
\qquad
u_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_\phi).
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $y_i$: the per-phase deficit. It is the same measurement as $d$, but with the phase index kept instead of averaged away.
  \item $u_p$: one offset per phase slice, eight in total.
  \item $\sigma_\phi$: the spread of those eight offsets. \textbf{This is the estimand for $H2$}, and it answers the question with a single number. If the deficit depends on where in its cycle the signal starts, the offsets differ and $\sigma_\phi$ is large. If the geometry alone decides it, the offsets are about equal and $\sigma_\phi$ is close to zero.
  \item $\beta_c$, $u_k$: as in Model~A, so that the phase term does not absorb differences between geometries or harmonics.
\end{itemize}

$H2$ predicts $\sigma_\phi \approx 0$. We use slices rather than a fitted curve because slices assume nothing about the \emph{shape} of a possible dependence: any systematic variation with phase makes the offsets differ.

\subsection{$H3$: where the dips are, and whether they move}
This is the second extension. It needs two models because $H3$ makes two claims. Both are fitted to the token collapse $z_g(f)$, the dispersion of the input-patch embeddings across patches, which is zero exactly when consecutive patches are identical. We sweep it on one shared grid: a uniform $1$\,Hz sweep joined with the predicted sites of \emph{every} configuration. In this way no geometry is scored only where its own theory predicts a dip, and non-integer sites such as $42.\overline{6}$\,Hz for $S{=}12$ are covered.

\emph{Which grid are the dips on?} Every swept frequency carries two yes/no labels: whether it lies on the stride grid $\{c f_s/S\}$, and whether it lies on the patch grid $\{k f_s/P\}$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2sites}
\log z_g(f) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
\alpha_g + \theta_S \cdot \text{OnStrideGrid} + \theta_P \cdot \text{OnPatchGrid},\ \sigma
\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $z_g(f)$: the dispersion of the patch embeddings across patches, at frequency $f$ for geometry $g$. We take the logarithm so that the two labels act multiplicatively.
  \item $\alpha_g$: the off-grid level for that geometry, which differs between models and is not of interest here.
  \item $\theta_S$: how much lower the dispersion is on the stride grid than off it. \textbf{This is the estimand}, and $H3$ predicts $\theta_S<0$.
  \item $\theta_P$: the same for the patch grid, so that the two families compete instead of one being assumed.
\end{itemize}

We also fit each label on its own, and once with neither, which shows which grid explains the dips. Only the $S<P$ geometries can settle this, because at $P{=}S$ the two grids fall on the same frequencies.

\emph{Do the dips move with the geometry?} A good fit above is still compatible with dips that only happen to coincide with a fixed grid. We therefore regress the measured spacing on the two predicted spacings:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:d2move}
\hat\Delta_g \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(
\kappa_0 + \kappa_S \frac{f_s}{S_g} + \kappa_P \frac{f_s}{P_g},\ \sigma
\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\hat\Delta_g$: the spacing read off the detected dips, without assuming what produced them.
  \item $\kappa_S$: the slope on the spacing predicted by the stride. \textbf{This is the estimand}, and $H3$ predicts exactly $1$.
  \item $\kappa_P$: the slope on the spacing predicted by the patch, which $H3$ predicts to be $0$.
  \item $\kappa_0$: an offset, predicted to be $0$.
\end{itemize}

There are two caveats. First, $P$ varies only along $P{=}S$, so $\kappa_P$ is confounded and the series at fixed $P{=}16$ carries the answer. Second, we measure the spacing in two ways, as the lowest dip and as the median gap, and fit both, because one spurious detection halves the median while the lowest dip is unaffected.

\subsection{Decisions and validation}
The thresholds are fixed before we see the posterior: the probability of at least $20\%$ attenuation, $\Pr(\bar\beta<\log0.8\mid D)$, and the equivalence regions $|\bar\beta|<\log1.1$, $\sigma_\phi<\log1.1$ and $|\kappa_S-1|<0.1$. A hypothesis is supported at probability ${\ge}0.95$, refuted at ${\le}0.05$, and \emph{inconclusive} in between. A pass/fail test cannot give that third outcome, which is often the honest answer here. Competing formulations are compared by leave-one-out cross-validation.

We validate before we interpret. Prior predictive checks come before collection. A recovery check then simulates data with a known effect and confirms that the model finds it, which separates ``no effect'' from ``a design that cannot see one''. Sampling uses NUTS, and we report a fit only when $\hat R<1.01$, the effective sample sizes are above $1000$, and there are no divergences.
